# Thesis writing

Preserve meaning and voice. Revise where the requested task, clarity, cohesion,
or accuracy warrants it; keep effective prose. Brevity helps only when it reduces
the reader's effort. Difficult ideas may need examples, space, and more words.

## Establish the project

Read applicable `AGENTS.md` or `CLAUDE.md` instructions and the project's
`THESIS.md`, if present. Resolve its paths relative to that file, not this skill.
Use the author's actual audience, discipline, language, manuscript, literature,
and supervisor feedback. If no brief exists, inspect the relevant project files
and state necessary assumptions; do not require a particular folder layout.

For an unspecified audience, offer an accessible orientation, a main explanation
for a newcomer to the field, and specialist detail sufficient to assess the
work. Do not impose an engineering audience on every thesis.

## Read selectively

- For prose, chapter structure, claims, captions, and reviews, read the relevant
  parts of [manuscript.md](references/manuscript.md). Read it fully for a broad rewrite.
- For source authority, extraction defects, or exact verification, read
  [sources.md](references/sources.md).
- For experimental design, literature synthesis, citation auditing, LaTeX
  mechanics, or a defense, use the corresponding thesis skill if installed.
  Those skills are optional; apply the relevant evidence rules here regardless.

## Build the argument

1. Establish the deliverable and baseline. Read actual feedback rather than
   inferring it from a rewrite. An outline request calls for a proposal; an
   authorized rewrite does not need a separate approval step.
2. For substantial restructuring, map reader questions and takeaways before
   polishing. Plan each section's opening, sequence of ideas, and ending.
   Give essential concepts a first explanation and later applications. A local
   wording edit does not need a chapter plan.
3. Connect the problem, research gap, proposed idea, comparisons, evidence, and
   implications. Distinguish borrowed methods, adaptations, and contributions.
4. Motivate unfamiliar ideas with concrete examples before naming or formalizing
   them. Explain a component's purpose, input, and output before its internals;
   explain the question before the metric. Teach prerequisites before use.
5. Develop connected paragraphs with varied sentence lengths. Make transitions
   explain the next question. Reuse useful examples without restarting them.
   Keep essential reasoning in the text and routine records in appendices.
6. Check new, substantively changed, unsupported, or questioned factual claims
   within the requested scope. Search the configured literature collection,
   read the relevant passage in context, and match evidence to claim scope.
   Reuse verified support when source, passage, and claim scope are unchanged.
7. Tie original findings to actual artifacts. A plan, implementation, simulation,
   and completed experiment establish different things; a commit message is not
   a scientific result. Keep excluded or deferred work outside the storyline.
8. Check terminology, captions, references, and comprehension. Return the
   requested text or review with substantive choices and unresolved evidence gaps.

## Preserve precision

Use verbs that match the evidence: `shows`, `suggests`, `is designed to`, and
`improves` are different claims. Keep qualifications beside the claims they
limit. Do not generalize across populations, tasks, or settings without support.
Never invent sources, results, quotations, or completed verification.

Quality-checked Markdown is the normal reading copy. Cite the original work.
Check the PDF for exact quotations, numbers, equations, layout-dependent content,
uncertain reference mappings, or extraction defects. Do not reopen every PDF
for an ordinary wording change.

These are adaptable writing defaults. Current author instructions and applicable
institutional requirements govern the task; local feedback is not a universal
rule for another thesis.
